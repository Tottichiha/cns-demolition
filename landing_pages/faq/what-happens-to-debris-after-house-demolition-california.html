California Demolition FAQ

What Happens to Debris After House Demolition in California?

Where demolition debris goes, what California law requires for recycling, and how to salvage materials you want to keep.

When an excavator knocks down a house, what happens to the resulting pile of wood, concrete, drywall, metal, and miscellaneous material? In California, the answer is governed by a specific set of environmental regulations — and your demolition contractor is responsible for ensuring debris is handled lawfully and documented properly.

California's Debris Diversion Requirement

AB 939 and local C&D ordinances: California's Integrated Waste Management Act (AB 939) requires most jurisdictions to achieve 75% diversion of waste from landfill. For construction and demolition debris specifically, most Southern California cities require 65–75% of C&D debris to be recycled or reused rather than landfilled. Your demolition contractor must submit a Waste Management Plan (WMP) before work begins describing how they will meet this threshold, and a C&D Diversion Report with disposal receipts afterward documenting where materials actually went.

If your city requires a WMP and your contractor cannot produce one, that is a red flag. It may indicate they are disposing of debris illegally — which can create liability for the property owner if debris is traced back to the address.

Where Demolition Debris Actually Goes

Recycled / Diverted

  • Concrete and masonry (crushed for road base)
  • Steel rebar and structural metal
  • Clean dimensional lumber
  • Copper plumbing and wiring
  • Aluminum windows and frames
  • Salvaged fixtures and doors

Licensed C&D Landfill

  • Drywall / gypsum wallboard
  • Mixed roofing materials
  • Insulation (non-asbestos)
  • Contaminated wood
  • Composite / engineered wood products
  • Plumbing fixtures (non-salvageable)

Licensed Hazmat Facility

  • Asbestos-containing materials (double-bagged)
  • Lead-contaminated debris
  • Fluorescent light tubes / bulbs
  • PCB-containing materials
  • Mercury-containing devices

The Role of Sorting on-Site vs. at the Facility

There are two approaches to debris diversion: sorting on-site during demolition (source separation) or hauling mixed loads to a "mixed C&D processing facility" that sorts mechanically. Both can satisfy California's diversion requirements, though source separation — where concrete goes in one bin, metal in another, wood in a third — typically achieves higher diversion rates and may reduce your disposal costs because clean materials command better prices at recyclers.

Ask your demolition contractor which approach they use and what diversion percentage they typically achieve. A contractor who cannot answer this question has probably not thought carefully about compliance.

Can You Salvage Items Before Demolition?

Yes — but you must arrange it before the excavator arrives

Once a machine demolition begins, salvage is no longer possible. If you want to keep items from your home, they must be removed during a separate "soft demo" or deconstruction phase before machine demolition. Items commonly worth salvaging include:

  • Hardwood flooring (especially old-growth Douglas fir, heart pine, or oak)
  • Solid wood doors and original hardware
  • Old-growth redwood or cedar framing lumber
  • Vintage light fixtures, plumbing fixtures, and cabinetry
  • Fireplace mantels and decorative millwork
  • Brick or stone from chimneys or foundations

Salvaged materials count toward your C&D diversion requirement and reduce the volume going to disposal, which lowers hauling costs. The tradeoff is additional labor time for the soft demo phase.

What Documentation Should You Receive?

At the end of a properly managed California demolition project, you should receive:

  • Disposal facility receipts (gate tickets) showing the weight of materials delivered to each facility and the facility's permit number
  • Recycler receipts confirming tonnage of concrete, metal, and wood delivered
  • C&D Diversion Report calculated from the receipts, showing your total tonnage and diversion percentage
  • Hazmat manifests for any asbestos or lead debris — these are legally required chain-of-custody documents

Keep these records. If your property is ever subject to an environmental audit or if the city requires proof of diversion as a condition of a future building permit, these documents are your evidence of compliance.

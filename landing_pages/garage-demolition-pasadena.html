What Makes Pasadena Garage Demo Different

A garage demo in Pasadena isn't the same job as pulling down a 1990s stucco structure in the Valley. Bungalow Heaven, Bungalow Club District, the Madison Heights Historic Overlay, and the Old Pasadena-adjacent residential blocks are full of structures that pre-date World War I. The garage out back isn't an afterthought — it was purpose-built alongside the house, using the same materials, the same lead-paint primers, and in many cases the same asbestos-containing roofing products that were standard from roughly 1910 to 1980.

Before a single wall comes down, the City of Pasadena Building & Safety requires a demolition permit, and SCAQMD Rule 1403 requires a regulated-materials survey by a California-certified asbestos inspector. For a pre-1920 structure, you should plan on finding asbestos-containing materials in the roofing, the floor tile if any was added in the 1940s–60s, any pipe insulation, and sometimes the original siding or sheathing. None of that makes the project impossible — it just means proper abatement runs ahead of the wrecking bar, not after it.

Historic District Alert

If your property is within a City of Pasadena Historic District or contains a structure individually listed on the Historic Register, the garage may itself qualify as a "contributing structure." In that case, the Historic Preservation Commission may require a Certificate of Appropriateness before Building & Safety will issue the demo permit. This is not a rumor — it happens on alley-facing carriage houses in Bungalow Heaven and in the Arroyo Seco neighborhoods west of Orange Grove Boulevard. We flag this at the estimate stage so there are no surprises after you've already cleared your schedule for demolition week.

The alley access question matters too. Many blocks between Mentor Avenue and Holliston Avenue, and throughout the Oak Knoll and Madison Heights neighborhoods, have alley-accessed garages set 20–30 feet back from the rear property line. Equipment routing — where the excavator enters, where the debris box sits, how spoil is loaded — needs to be confirmed before any crew mobilizes. Alleys in this part of Pasadena are narrow, sometimes unpaved, and occasionally shared with mature trees the City may have opinions about.

SCAQMD Rule 1403: What the Survey Actually Finds

SCAQMD Rule 1403 — No Age Exemption

Rule 1403 applies to all structures regardless of when they were built. A post-1979 garage still requires a pre-demolition asbestos survey — the rule has no cutoff date. What changes as a structure gets older is the expected survey yield: pre-1920 buildings in Pasadena almost universally test positive for regulated materials in at least one building component.

A certified asbestos inspector samples every suspect material category: roofing felts and mastics, floor tiles and adhesives, wall and ceiling plaster, pipe wrap and duct insulation, window glazing compound, caulks, and exterior siding products. The lab analysis determines if any sample exceeds the 1% asbestos threshold. If it does, licensed abatement must be completed and documented before demo begins — and SCAQMD must receive written notification at least 10 working days in advance of demolition.

For a 1918 carriage house, expect: positive roofing (chrysotile in the original felt layers), possible positive floor tile if 1940s–60s tile was added, and high probability of positive window glazing. Each positive component gets abated as a separate line item. We coordinate with our licensed abatement subs and fold the survey cost into your complete project quote.

Lead paint is a parallel concern. California's RRP Rule (similar to EPA 40 CFR Part 745) governs lead-based paint disturbance during demolition. Pre-1940 structures should be assumed to have lead paint on all painted surfaces — exterior siding, window sashes, doors, and interior trim. Our crew works with appropriate respiratory protection, and debris is managed as lead-contaminated material throughout.

The Step-by-Step Process in Pasadena

Here's how a Pasadena garage demo actually moves from call to cleared pad — no glossed-over steps.

1

Site Assessment & Historic District Check

We confirm your APN against Pasadena's Historic Resource Inventory and active historic overlay zones. If the garage is a potential contributing structure, we flag it before you spend money on a survey. If you're in the clear, we move to Step 2.

2

SCAQMD Rule 1403 Survey

A California-certified asbestos inspector samples all suspect materials. Lab turnaround is typically 3–5 business days. If regulated materials are found, we prepare the SCAQMD notification (10-working-day requirement) and bring in licensed abatement. If the structure is clean, we proceed directly to permit application.

3

Utility Disconnection Coordination

For electric and water service, you contact Pasadena Water and Power (PWP) — not SCE, not LADWP. PWP is the city's municipal utility and handles both. For gas, that's SoCalGas. We give you the exact disconnect request language and help you sequence the calls so nothing drags the schedule. Utility disconnects typically run 2–4 weeks ahead of demo; we account for this in the project timeline.

4

Pasadena Building & Safety Permit

A complete demolition permit application goes to Building & Safety at 175 N. Garfield Avenue. For a straightforward detached garage with no historic complications, expect 4–6 weeks for permit issuance on a complete application. If HPC review is required, that process runs concurrently with early-stage prep work.

5

Equipment Access & Alley Routing Confirmed

Alley-access garages throughout Bungalow Heaven, Madison Heights, and the Cleveland-to-Lake Avenues corridor require on-site verification before we schedule equipment. Alley width, surface condition, overhead lines, and tree canopy all factor into whether a skid steer versus a mini-excavator is the right call, and where the debris box sits without creating a neighbor dispute.

6

Demolition & Debris Removal

Once abatement is documented, utilities are disconnected, and the permit is posted, the physical demo is typically a one-day job for a standard detached garage. We sort concrete rubble from wood debris on site for separate disposal. The pad is graded level or per your spec if an ADU or new structure follows.

7

Final Inspection & Permit Close

Building & Safety requires a final inspection to close the permit. We schedule it, we're on site for it, and we handle any punch-list items the inspector notes. You receive the closed permit documentation for your records — important if you're applying for an ADU permit on the same parcel immediately after.

Pasadena Utilities — Who to Call

The most common mistake homeowners make is calling the wrong utility. Pasadena has its own municipal utility — nothing in this city goes through LADWP or SCE.

Electric & Water

Pasadena Water and Power (PWP) — the city's municipal utility. Handles both electric disconnection and water service shutoff. Request demo disconnects through PWP, not through SoCal Edison or LADWP.

Natural Gas

SoCalGas handles gas service disconnection in Pasadena. Schedule your gas shutoff and cap separately from PWP. Both disconnects need to be documented before the Building & Safety inspector signs off.

Building Permits

City of Pasadena Building & Safety, 175 N. Garfield Ave. Complete applications: 4–6 weeks. Incomplete applications restart the clock. We submit a complete package the first time.

Historic Review

Historic Preservation Commission, City of Pasadena. Required if the structure is a contributing element in a designated historic district. Commission schedules monthly — timing your application matters.

Tearing Down to Build an ADU

Garage demolition in Pasadena increasingly goes hand-in-hand with ADU construction. The City actively encourages ADU development and has one of the more streamlined ADU approval processes in the San Gabriel Valley. If you're demolishing a detached garage to build a new ADU in its footprint, there are a few things worth knowing before you start.

Under California Government Code Section 65852.2, a local agency cannot require you to replace the parking spaces being eliminated when a garage is converted to or replaced by an ADU. This is state law, and it applies in Pasadena regardless of how many parking spaces your zoning normally requires. You cannot be denied an ADU permit on the basis that you're losing garage parking — full stop.

The sequencing matters. The demolition permit and the ADU construction permit are separate applications, but they're often submitted in parallel. Building & Safety can process them concurrently, which compresses the overall timeline. We time our demo scope so the pad is compacted and ready for your ADU contractor to start foundation work immediately after the permit inspection closes. If the ADU design calls for a specific grading elevation or a particular compaction depth, tell us at the estimate stage and we build it into the scope.

One thing that catches people off guard: if the existing garage has any historic designation and you're proposing to replace it with a new ADU structure, HPC may want to review the new design as well — not just the demolition. This is relatively uncommon but worth raising with your architect early.

Questions We Actually Get Asked

Does a garage in Bungalow Heaven or another Pasadena historic district need separate historic review before demolition?

It depends on whether the garage is considered a "contributing structure" within the district. Bungalow Heaven (bounded roughly by Washington Boulevard, Hill Avenue, Orange Grove Boulevard, and Marengo Avenue) is a City-designated historic district, and many of the original alley-facing carriage houses within it are considered contributing elements — meaning they add to the historic character that earned the district its designation. If that's your situation, Building & Safety will require a Certificate of Appropriateness from the Historic Preservation Commission before it issues the demolition permit. HPC meets monthly and applications need to be submitted at least a few weeks before the meeting. The process isn't guaranteed to say yes, either — sometimes HPC wants alternatives to full demolition explored. This is why we flag the historic district question at the very first site visit rather than after the survey is already paid for.

The SCAQMD survey for a 1918 carriage house — what should I expect to find?

Honestly? Expect positives in at least two or three material categories. Roofing is almost always the first hit — the original roofing felts used from roughly 1910 through the 1940s were typically chrysotile-reinforced, and the mastic used to apply them was commonly asbestos-containing as well. If any flooring was added during the 1940s–1960s renovation craze, nine-inch or twelve-inch floor tiles are a known hazard and their adhesives more so. Window glazing compound on original wood windows tests positive frequently. Pipe wrap on any gas or water lines that run through the structure is another common find. None of this stops the project — it just adds abatement scope and the SCAQMD 10-working-day notification timeline. A certified inspector's full survey takes about half a day on a standard carriage house and the lab turnaround runs 3–5 business days. We don't skip this step and we don't subcontract it to the cheapest surveyor we can find; the documentation needs to be clean when SCAQMD asks for it.

We want to convert the garage to an ADU, not demolish it — does that change anything with the permit process?

Yes, meaningfully. A garage conversion (keeping the existing walls and structure) is an ADU construction permit, not a demolition permit — Building & Safety routes it differently. The SCAQMD survey obligation also shifts: you're not demolishing, so Rule 1403's pre-demolition notification requirement doesn't apply in the same way, though any work that disturbs suspected ACMs during the renovation still triggers separate requirements. The historic district question also changes character — HPC may be far more comfortable approving a conversion that preserves the original carriage house exterior than it would be approving outright demolition. If preservation of the historic fabric is possible without blowing your budget, it's worth the conversation. That said, original carriage houses built in 1918 often have foundation issues, substandard framing by modern code standards, and so much deferred maintenance that conversion costs can exceed new construction. We'll give you an honest read on what we see structurally when we walk the site.

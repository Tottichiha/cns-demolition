Pasadena Is Not LA — and That Changes Everything About How Demolition Works

Property owners who have dealt with demolition permits elsewhere in Los Angeles County often come to Pasadena expecting the same process. They are wrong, and that assumption costs them weeks. Pasadena is an independent city. Its Building & Safety Division does not run through LA County. Its water and electric do not go through LADWP or SCE for most addresses — they run through Pasadena Water and Power, a city-owned municipal utility that operates on its own disconnection schedule and its own request process.

That is the straightforward part. The more complex issue is Pasadena's historic preservation overlay. A house does not have to be on the National Register of Historic Places — or even formally designated by the city — to trigger review before your demolition permit moves forward. If your property sits within one of Pasadena's locally designated historic districts, or if Pasadena Heritage identifies it as potentially significant, the city's Historic Preservation Commission enters the picture. What was supposed to be a 4-to-6-week plan check becomes something longer, and more expensive, if you file before understanding what you are actually dealing with.

We have run whole-house demolitions throughout Pasadena's older neighborhoods — Bungalow Heaven, Prospect Park, Madison Heights, Oak Knoll, Linda Vista, and Hastings Ranch. The projects go smoothly when the permit sequence is right. They stall when someone files for a demolition permit without first confirming their historic district status and lining up their utility disconnections correctly. This page covers what we know from doing it, not from reading about it.

Historic Districts and the Mills Act: Two Issues That Kill Demolition Timelines

Pasadena has four locally designated historic districts where demolition triggers mandatory review by the Historic Preservation Commission: Bungalow Heaven (northeast Pasadena, bounded roughly by Washington Boulevard on the south, Mentor Avenue on the west, Orange Grove Boulevard on the north, and Hill Avenue on the east), Prospect Park (near California Boulevard and Wilson Avenue, adjacent to Caltech), Madison Heights, and the San Rafael Hills area. If your property is in one of these districts, you likely need a Certificate of Demolition from the Historic Preservation Commission before Building & Safety will even accept your permit application.

The Mills Act problem is separate and more serious. The Mills Act is a California program that gives owners of qualified historic properties a significant property tax reduction in exchange for a contractual obligation to maintain the property's historic character. If your home has a Mills Act contract with the City of Pasadena, demolition is a breach of that contract. The financial penalties are real, and the city can pursue legal remedies. This is not a building code issue you resolve with a variance — it is a contractual and legal question that requires an attorney before a single permit application is filed.

The tell for Mills Act properties: the assessed value on your property tax bill will show a significantly lower value than comparable homes on the street. If you bought the property and did not receive disclosure of a Mills Act contract, that is a separate real estate issue. Either way, check before you demo.

The Actual Permit Sequence in Pasadena — Step by Step

Every whole-house demolition in Pasadena follows roughly the same regulatory path. The timeline depends on how cleanly each step is handled, not on how fast the city is. Incomplete applications and missing surveys are the two most common sources of delay, and both are preventable.

1

Confirm Historic District Status and Mills Act Status

Before anything else is scheduled, this question gets answered. Check Pasadena's GIS historic resource map and call Planning & Community Development if there is any ambiguity. Mills Act status shows up in the county assessor's records. This step takes a day, not weeks — do it first.

2

SCAQMD Rule 1403 Asbestos Survey

South Coast Air Quality Management District Rule 1403 requires an asbestos survey by a licensed asbestos consultant (LAC) before any demolition permit can be issued. There is no age exemption — even a newly built structure requires the survey, though the practical risk for newer homes is low. For Pasadena's stock of 1910s through 1930s construction — Craftsman bungalows with lath-and-plaster walls, vinyl floor tile over original hardwood, pipe insulation, and old roofing materials — a positive result for asbestos is not a surprise. It is the expected outcome. The survey dictates what abatement work must happen before the wrecking crew sets up, and the abatement must be documented before the City of Pasadena Building & Safety Division will issue the demolition permit.

3

Historic Preservation Commission Review (If Applicable)

For properties in designated historic districts, the Certificate of Demolition application goes to the Historic Preservation Commission. This includes submitting documentation on why demolition is appropriate, often with evidence that rehabilitation is not economically feasible. Commission meetings are scheduled — missing a filing deadline means waiting for the next cycle. Pasadena Heritage, the city's independent preservation advocacy organization, monitors permit activity and may provide comment on applications they consider significant. Their involvement is not a permit block, but it can extend the review period and introduce community opposition that the Commission weighs.

4

Demolition Permit Application — City of Pasadena Building & Safety Division

With the asbestos survey complete, any historic review resolved, and the demolition plan drawn, the permit application goes to Building & Safety. For complete, compliant applications, plan check typically runs four to six weeks. Pasadena Building & Safety is an independent city operation — do not confuse their process, their fees, or their inspectors with LA County Building and Safety, which handles unincorporated areas and other municipalities.

5

Pasadena Water and Power Disconnection

PWP handles both water and electric service for most Pasadena addresses. They require a disconnection request that references your permit number, and they schedule the meter pull and water service cap on their own timeline — typically one to two weeks out from request. This step runs concurrently with permit issuance, not after it, but the permit number is required to initiate the PWP request. Coordinate early. SoCalGas handles the gas line separately and runs their own disconnection appointment process on a parallel track.

6

Demolition and Foundation Removal or Cap

Once utilities are confirmed off, inspected, and signed off, the structure comes down. Depending on what is planned for the site — new construction, subdivision, or hold — the foundation is either removed to grade or left in place per the new building plan. Site grading, erosion controls, and haul-out all happen in this phase. We handle the city's final inspection scheduling and the documentation closeout.

Pre-1940 Pasadena Construction and Asbestos — What to Actually Expect

Pasadena's historic neighborhoods are lined with Craftsman bungalows and Spanish Colonial Revival homes built between roughly 1905 and 1940. These are architecturally significant structures, and they are also construction products of their era — which means asbestos is embedded in the building fabric in ways that are not visible from the street or even from a standard walk-through.

The typical hazmat profile for a pre-1940 Pasadena home: asbestos-containing plaster (both the base coat and finish coat), vinyl floor tile containing chrysotile asbestos installed over original hardwood, pipe insulation on galvanized or cast-iron runs, roofing felt and cementitious shingles, and occasionally transite panels on older additions or detached garages. The SCAQMD Rule 1403 survey samples each of these suspect materials, and each positive sample requires licensed abatement before demolition begins.

"Abatement is not the obstacle people expect it to be — it is a scheduled, documented process. What creates project delays is discovering asbestos after permit issuance and having to re-sequence the work. The survey eliminates that variable."

Lead paint is the second standard concern in this construction era, though the regulatory requirements differ from asbestos. Pasadena Building & Safety does not require a lead survey as a permit condition the way SCAQMD mandates the asbestos survey, but Cal/OSHA's lead standard applies to the workers doing the demolition. We follow the required practices for lead-containing materials and document accordingly.

Demolition Considerations by Pasadena Neighborhood

Pasadena is not a uniform city. Historic designation, construction era, and lot configuration vary significantly across its neighborhoods. Here is what matters for whole-house demolition in the areas we work most frequently.

Historic District

Bungalow Heaven

Northeast Pasadena, roughly Washington to Orange Grove, Mentor to Hill. Heavily concentrated 1910s–1920s Craftsman bungalows. Locally designated historic district — demolition requires Historic Preservation Commission review and a Certificate of Demolition. Pasadena Heritage is highly active here.

Historic District

Prospect Park

Near California Boulevard and Wilson Avenue, adjacent to Caltech's east side. Mix of Craftsman, Mediterranean Revival, and Colonial Revival. Locally designated district. Strong neighborhood advocacy. Demo applications in this area typically draw Pasadena Heritage commentary.

Historic District

Madison Heights

Central-west Pasadena, primarily 1920s–1930s Spanish Colonial Revival and Period Revival construction. Locally designated historic district. Larger lot sizes than Bungalow Heaven; some teardown-rebuild activity, which means the Commission review process is more established here.

Non-Designated / Watch Area

Oak Knoll

South Pasadena border area with significant pre-war estate construction. Not a designated historic district, but individual properties frequently carry Mills Act contracts or individual landmark designations. Always verify property status before assuming straightforward permit processing.

Non-Designated

Linda Vista

Hillside neighborhood northwest of the Rose Bowl. Varied construction eras; some mid-century modern alongside older stock. Less historic designation pressure than central Pasadena, but hillside grading requirements, haul route logistics, and utility access add project complexity.

Non-Designated

Hastings Ranch

East Pasadena, largely post-war construction from the 1950s and 1960s. Lower historic review risk than the older central neighborhoods. Asbestos in floor tile and insulation is still a realistic expectation. Straightforward permit process when the SCAQMD survey is handled upfront.

Pasadena Water and Power — What Makes PWP Different

One of the most consistent sources of confusion on Pasadena jobs is the utility question. Most of the Los Angeles basin runs through LADWP for water and electric, with SCE handling electric in surrounding cities. Pasadena is an exception that surprises people every time.

Pasadena Water and Power is a municipally owned utility — owned by the City of Pasadena — and it operates both the water system and the electric grid for the majority of the city. You do not call LADWP. You do not call SCE. You call PWP, and they coordinate both services through their own customer service and field operations.

The practical implication for demolition: your meter pull and water cap requests go to PWP, reference your City of Pasadena demolition permit number, and get scheduled on PWP's timeline. Their scheduling is generally predictable, but the request must be initiated with the correct documentation. A call to LADWP or SCE solves nothing — they do not serve most Pasadena addresses and will tell you so.

SoCalGas serves Pasadena for natural gas, and their disconnection process runs independently. Both agencies — PWP and SoCalGas — need confirmed disconnections with signed-off inspections before demolition begins. We coordinate both as part of the project timeline.

What Pasadena Heritage Actually Does

Pasadena Heritage is a nonprofit historic preservation advocacy organization, not a city agency. It does not issue permits, and it does not have veto authority over demolition applications. What it does have is standing to provide comment to the Historic Preservation Commission, community credibility, and the organizational capacity to draw attention to demolition applications it considers significant losses.

For most demolition projects in Pasadena, Pasadena Heritage is not a factor. Their attention tends to focus on properties with documented historical or architectural significance — early works by notable architects, properties associated with historical events, or structures that represent an increasingly rare building type in the neighborhood.

Where they do engage, their involvement can extend the HPC review period and, occasionally, result in a Commission decision to deny the Certificate of Demolition. An appeal process exists, but it adds cost and time. The way to avoid that outcome is to have the full picture before filing — knowing whether your property is on their radar, and if so, preparing a thorough application that addresses the Commission's criteria for demolition approval.

This is not a reason to avoid demolition in Pasadena. It is a reason to do the groundwork first.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if my Pasadena home is in a historic district or subject to demolition review?

Pasadena maintains several locally designated historic districts — Bungalow Heaven, Prospect Park, Madison Heights, and others. If your property falls within one of these districts, the Historic Preservation Commission may require a Certificate of Demolition before Building & Safety will issue a demolition permit. You can look up your property on Pasadena's zoning and historic resource maps through the city's GIS portal, or call the Planning & Community Development Department at City Hall directly. Even properties outside formal district boundaries can trigger informal review if Pasadena Heritage flags them as architecturally significant. Pre-1940 Craftsman bungalows and Spanish Colonial Revival homes are the most common candidates. When we assess a Pasadena project, confirming historic status is the first conversation we have — before any permit discussion begins.

What does the Mills Act have to do with demolition?

The Mills Act is a California program that allows owners of qualified historic properties to receive a property tax reduction — often substantial — in exchange for a contractual commitment to maintain and preserve the property's historic character. If your Pasadena property carries a Mills Act contract, demolition is not simply a permit question. It is a contract breach with financial penalties and potential legal action by the city. You would need to formally terminate the contract, which typically requires repayment of accumulated tax savings and involves the Pasadena City Attorney's office. This is not resolved by pulling a demolition permit — the permit may actually be denied while the contract is active. Never proceed with demolition planning on a Mills Act property without a real estate attorney reviewing the contract terms first. The tell: if your property tax assessed value looks significantly lower than comparable homes on the street, look closely at why.

Does Pasadena Water and Power handle the utility disconnection, or is it SCE or LADWP?

For most Pasadena addresses, Pasadena Water and Power handles both water and electric service. This catches people off guard because the rest of the LA region runs through LADWP for water and electric, or SCE for electric in surrounding municipalities. PWP is a city-owned utility operating entirely within Pasadena's service territory. For demolition, you submit a disconnection request to PWP that references your Building & Safety permit number, and they schedule the meter pull and water cap through their field operations team. SoCalGas handles natural gas lines on a separate track and requires its own disconnection appointment. Coordinating PWP and SoCalGas in the right sequence — and getting both confirmed off and inspected before the structure comes down — is part of what we manage on every Pasadena project.

What You're Actually Dealing With in a Pre-War Pasadena Home

Pasadena's older residential neighborhoods — Bungalow Heaven along the streets north of Washington Boulevard, the larger Craftsman homes in Prospect Park, the Spanish Colonial Revivals in Madison Heights and Oak Knoll — were built in a concentrated window from roughly 1905 through the late 1930s. The construction methods of that era produced homes of genuine quality and genuine complexity when it comes time to renovate them.

The walls in a 1918 Pasadena Craftsman are not drywall. They are wood lath — thin strips of wood nailed horizontally across the studs — covered with three coats of plaster: scratch coat, brown coat, and finish coat. That system is heavy, it's hard, and it does not come apart the way drywall does. A three-pound sledge that would drop a sheet of drywall in one swing might take three or four blows to break through original plaster that has cured for over a century. Debris volume and haul weight are both higher than a comparable drywall job — and every quote we give for a Pasadena pre-war home reflects that reality.

Then there is the wallpaper. In older Pasadena homes we routinely encounter three, four, sometimes five successive layers of wallpaper applied directly over the plaster. Each owner over the decades added their own and papered over what came before. By the time we get there, that assembly — paper over paper over paper over plaster over lath — is essentially a laminated panel. It has to come off as part of interior demo, and it adds time.

The original hardwood floors are a different calculation entirely. Douglas fir or oak strip flooring from the 1910s and 1920s is often in better shape than anything you could buy today, and Pasadena homeowners frequently want it salvaged. Salvage changes the demo sequence: the floors have to be protected or carefully removed before any overhead or wall demo begins. If you decide mid-demolition that you want to save the floors, the sequence has already been broken. We work through this in the site visit, not on demo day.

Original Bathrooms: The Mortar-Bed Multiplier

Tile bathrooms in Pasadena homes from this era were set in mortar beds, not thin-set. A mortar-bed tile installation — the tile, the mortar bed, the wire lath beneath it — can weigh 25 to 40 pounds per square foot. An 80-square-foot bathroom can generate 2,000 to 3,000 pounds of dense, awkward debris from the floor alone before you've touched a single wall. We account for this specifically in bathroom demo quotes. A flat per-room rate that treats a mortar-bed bathroom the same as a modern thin-set job is either wrong or it carries a hidden markup somewhere else.

Original cast iron drain lines are present in most pre-war Pasadena homes and require careful handling. They're typically not part of demo scope, but they intersect demo scope at every bathroom and kitchen wall. We mark them during the site visit and work around them.

Health & Safety Notice

Lead Paint: Present Until Proven Absent in Any Pasadena Home Built Before 1940

Lead-based paint was used universally in residential construction before 1940. In Pasadena, that covers the entirety of Bungalow Heaven, Prospect Park, and Madison Heights as they were predominantly built. C&S treats lead as present until a certified inspector proves otherwise — and even then, our crews work in containment when demo generates fine dust from any pre-1940 surface. This isn't a legal hedge; it's the correct protocol for a pre-war Craftsman where you genuinely do not know how many paint layers have been applied over what base. The first repaint of an old Pasadena home was quite possibly also lead paint, just in a different color. California RRP regulations apply to demo work that disturbs painted surfaces in pre-1978 homes, and we operate in compliance with those requirements on every job.

Asbestos in the Plaster and Wire in the Walls

The SCAQMD — the South Coast Air Quality Management District — requires a survey for asbestos-containing materials before any demolition that will disturb those materials. For pre-1945 construction in Pasadena, that survey must include the plaster itself. Some formulations of plaster used in California construction from the 1910s through the early 1940s incorporated asbestos fiber in the mix for strength and fire resistance. There is no way to identify this by visual inspection; a laboratory analysis of a plaster sample is the only method.

If the plaster tests positive for asbestos, abatement must be completed by a licensed asbestos contractor before any demolition of the plaster walls begins. C&S does not perform asbestos abatement — we are a demolition contractor — but we work regularly alongside licensed asbestos firms in Pasadena and the Foothill area, and we can coordinate the sequencing of that work with the overall demo schedule. The abatement happens first; once the surveyor has signed off, we proceed with structural demo. Trying to shortcut this sequence creates legal and safety exposure that no contractor should be willing to absorb, and we won't.

Knob-and-Tube Wiring: The Non-Negotiable Pre-Condition

Knob-and-tube electrical wiring — the original system used in Pasadena homes from the 1890s through approximately 1940 — runs through the wall cavities that our crews will open during interior demolition. C&S does not demolish walls containing live knob-and-tube wiring. This is not a preference. It is a hard stop.

The knob-and-tube in the demo zone must be de-energized and physically removed by a licensed electrician before our crew begins work in those walls. The electrician identifies the circuits involved, pulls the permits required by the City of Pasadena Building & Safety Division at City Hall on Garfield Avenue, de-energizes and removes the wiring from the affected cavities, and signs off. Then we demo. The sequence is not negotiable, and any contractor who tells you otherwise is putting your project at risk.

If you don't already have an electrician coordinated for this, we can refer you to firms that work regularly in Pasadena's older housing stock and understand the permit process at the local Building & Safety office. Scheduling that work ahead of demo day — not as an afterthought on the morning of — is the difference between a demo that proceeds on schedule and one that stalls for two weeks waiting for an inspection.

Permits, Historic Districts, and City of Pasadena Building & Safety

The permit picture for interior demo in Pasadena is nuanced. Cosmetic interior work — removing drywall or finishes without touching structural elements — typically does not require a standalone demolition permit from the City of Pasadena Building & Safety Division. However, the moment you remove a wall — even one that turns out to be non-load-bearing — or open the structure in a way that requires inspection, a permit is required. And the knob-and-tube electrical work that must precede demo absolutely requires an electrical permit, which is pulled by the licensed electrician performing that work.

Pasadena also has a historic preservation layer that affects some properties. The Bungalow Heaven Landmark District — bounded roughly by Washington Boulevard on the south, Orange Grove on the west, Mentor Avenue on the north, and Sunnyslope Avenue on the east — requires a Certificate of Appropriateness for exterior alterations, but interior work generally does not require that review. However, homes individually designated as City Landmarks or contributing structures to other historic districts may face additional review requirements even for interior work if it affects character-defining features. If your property has a historic designation, we'll flag it at the site visit and you should confirm scope with the Pasadena Department of Planning and Community Development before demo begins.

The practical effect: for a straightforward interior gut on a Bungalow Heaven property, permit requirements center on the electrical pre-clearance, not on the demolition itself. For a project that involves structural wall removal or mechanical system exposure requiring inspection, permits are pulled appropriately. We do not work unpermitted on jobs that require permits, and we have no interest in doing so — particularly in a city like Pasadena where inspectors know the older housing stock well and look for it.

Questions We Hear on Every Pasadena Job

Owners of pre-war Pasadena homes come to us with the same questions. Here are the honest answers.

Does knob-and-tube wiring have to be removed before interior demo can start?

Yes, in the demo zones — and this is not something we will compromise on. Knob-and-tube wiring runs through the wall cavities that will be opened during interior demolition. Demolishing a wall with live K&T inside it creates an immediate shock and fire hazard for the crew. The correct sequence is: licensed electrician pulls an electrical permit from the City of Pasadena Building & Safety, de-energizes the circuits in the demo zone, physically removes the knob-and-tube from those wall cavities, and gets the inspection sign-off. Then we start demo. We can refer you to electricians who do this work regularly in Pasadena's older homes if you don't already have one engaged, but that coordination needs to happen before we schedule the demo date — not the week of.

We want to salvage the original hardwood floors — can C&S work around them?

Yes, but the decision has to be made before demo day, not during. Salvage changes the sequence: floor protection or careful removal has to happen before any overhead demo begins, because debris from ceiling and wall work will fall directly onto exposed flooring. Once wall and ceiling demo is underway, protecting the floor retroactively is extremely difficult and usually unsuccessful. At the site visit, we'll assess the floor condition — original Douglas fir and oak in Pasadena Craftsman homes is frequently worth saving — and if you want it kept, we build the demo sequence around that. If you want certain rooms salvaged and others cleared entirely, we handle it room by room. Just make the call before we show up with a crew.

Does interior demo in a Pasadena historic district require any additional permits or review?

It depends on the specific designation and the scope of work. The Bungalow Heaven Landmark District primarily governs exterior alterations — the Certificate of Appropriateness process run by the Pasadena Historic Preservation Commission focuses on exterior character-defining features, not interior work. Straightforward interior gut work in a Bungalow Heaven home generally does not trigger that review process. However, if your property carries an individual City Landmark designation, or if the interior work involves character-defining interior features like original built-ins, beamed ceilings, or decorative tile that the designation specifically addresses, there may be additional review requirements. The safest approach is to confirm with the Pasadena Department of Planning and Community Development, located at City Hall on Garfield Avenue, before work begins. We flag any designations we're aware of at the site visit, but the homeowner or architect is responsible for confirming permit and review requirements with the city.
